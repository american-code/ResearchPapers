## Study 1: Mechanistic Circuit Analysis of Open-Weight LLMs on Apple Silicon

**Hypothesis.** Wang et al. (2022) identified a three-zone functional circuit for Indirect Object Identification (IOI) in GPT-2 Small: early duplicate-token heads that flag repeated names, mid S-inhibition heads that suppress the subject, and late name-mover heads that copy the indirect object to the output. The hypothesis driving this study was that this functional topology reflects a general algorithmic solution to the IOI problem — not an artifact of GPT-2's architecture — and should therefore reappear in modern large-scale models at corresponding relative depths, even when head indices, parameter counts, and attention mechanisms differ substantially.

**Infrastructure contribution.** All experiments were conducted using SwiftSci Interp, an activation-patching harness built on Apple's MLX framework that runs on Apple Silicon without GPU cluster access. The library implements a hook-compatible patching interface for LlamaForCausalLM and GPTNeoXForCausalLM by hot-swapping each attention module with a patchable drop-in at load time, enabling the full IOI patching sweep — 674 forward passes for Llama-3.2-3B, 386 for Pythia-1.4B — on consumer hardware in hours rather than days.

**IOI circuit findings.** Using activation patching (sufficiency) and mean ablation (necessity) on 100 IOI examples per model, a circuit-critical head was identified in each model that dominates all others. In Llama-3.2-3B, head L15H20 at relative depth 0.536 accounts for 27.9% of clean logit difference (ablation drop 1.578; 70% larger than rank-2). In Pythia-1.4B, L10H7 at depth 0.417 accounts for 21.7% of clean LD (ablation drop 0.893; 62% above rank-2). Both models exhibit a concentrated single-head bottleneck that is absent from Wang et al.'s distributed 26-component circuit in GPT-2 Small — a novel finding that may reflect scale effects or task-distribution differences. A dissociative pattern was observed in Pythia's L1H11: near-zero patching score (−0.003, CI straddles zero) alongside large ablation drop (0.327), identifying a suppression role — necessity without sufficiency — that a patching-only or ablation-only protocol would miss entirely.

**Cross-model comparison.** The three functional depth zones from GPT-2 Small are present in both modern models at compatible relative depths. Matching the top-10 heads in each model by relative depth (±0.075 tolerance), 8 of 10 positions are shared between Llama-3.2-3B and Pythia-1.4B. Six of the eight matched pairs differ by ≤0.02 in relative depth — substantially tighter than the matching tolerance. The two unmatched heads are interpretable: Llama has an additional output-adjustment head in its final layer (absent in the shallower Pythia), and Pythia has an early suppression head with no Llama analog. A cross-task comparison on factual association prompts in Llama further revealed that the top-5 factual association heads occupy the same five layers as five IOI heads — with different head indices within those layers — indicating layer-level circuit topology conservation across tasks, with head-level specialization by task.

## Study 2: SAE Cross-Architecture Comparison — Feature Universality and Evaluation Confounds

**Core question.** Three sparse autoencoder (SAE) training objective families — standard L1 with sparsity penalty, TopK with hard sparsity constraint, and Gated SAEs with separate detection and magnitude networks — have been reported to produce different levels of feature interpretability in prior work. However, no prior comparison has held all experimental conditions constant (model, training budget, sparsity level, evaluation protocol) while varying only the objective. This study asks two linked questions: which objective produces the most interpretable features, and whether prior comparative results reflect objective inductive bias or confounded experimental design.

**Universality findings.** SAEs were trained across three model families — GPT-2 Small, Pythia-1.4B, and Llama-3.2-3B — at a mid-network layer (relative depth ≈ 0.50), on 500M tokens from The Pile, using matched sparsity levels and identical training budgets on Apple Silicon M3 Max. Cross-architecture feature matching used Hungarian assignment over functional similarity (FunSim) — the cosine similarity of activation patterns over a shared 2.8M-token aligned evaluation corpus — which sidesteps the arbitrary choice of basis in each SAE's decoder space. Approximately 13–17% of features exceed the permutation-null threshold for cross-architecture universality: 15.1% for GPT-2 Small ↔ Pythia-1.4B, 13.3% for GPT-2 Small ↔ Llama, and 16.9% for Pythia ↔ Llama. Three-way universal features cluster strongly into structural linguistic categories — punctuation, determiners, possessives, numeric tokens, capitalization at sentence boundaries — rather than semantic categories. The top-ranked feature reaches FunSim ≥ 0.90 across all three pairwise comparisons, indicating near-perfect activation-pattern alignment across architectures trained on different corpora by different organizations.

**Evaluation confound.** The central quantitative finding is that the probe-accuracy advantage of TopK and Gated over standard L1 SAEs — the basis for prior claims that these objectives produce more interpretable features — is fully mediated by reconstruction quality. Regressing probe accuracy against variance explained (VarExp) within each model yields R² values of 0.87–0.91. After controlling for VarExp, objective type has no statistically significant effect on probe accuracy (β = 0.004, p = 0.61). The partial correlation between objective type and probe accuracy drops from r = 0.62 to r = 0.07 — an 89% reduction — after removing reconstruction variance. The mechanism is precise: TopK's hard cutoff achieves higher VarExp at matched L₀ because it avoids L1 shrinkage on active features, improving reconstruction without changing the number of active features. Prior evaluations that matched L₀ while reporting probe accuracy were inadvertently measuring reconstruction quality, not interpretability. The three metrics (probe accuracy, human interpretability ratings, steering fidelity) produce systematically different architecture rankings: TopK leads on probe accuracy, Gated on human ratings, and L1 is competitive on steering fidelity.

## Study 3: Distributed Mechanistic Interpretability — mlxMesh, Scale, and Safety Evaluation

**Contribution.** This study describes mlxMesh, a system that splits transformer inference across two Apple Silicon nodes connected by Thunderbolt, streams intermediate layer activations over a purpose-built binary wire protocol, and trains sparse autoencoders on the streaming output without storing activations to disk. The core infrastructure problem motivating the work is that the models worth understanding exceed single-node memory, while the hardware capable of running them is not designed for continuous activation capture. mlxMesh addresses this by making the split point and the activation tap the same operation: activations at the split boundary are transmitted over the wire, analyzed on the second node, and passed forward for continued inference.

The wire protocol (mlxMesh v1.0.0) uses an 8-byte header (layer index, token start, token count) plus float16 payload, with credit-based flow control that prevents the inference node from outrunning the SAE training pipeline. Protocol overhead is 0.20% at representative throughput — negligible at all model sizes. The distributed SAE trainer partitions the activation dataset across two workers that train independently and exchange gradient updates via weighted averaging, equivalent to synchronous data-parallel training.

**Scale achieved.** End-to-end validation on Llama-3.2-3B demonstrated zero reconstruction error at the split boundary (max_abs_error = 0.0 across all tensor positions), confirming that bfloat16→float16 conversion is lossless for activations in the observed range. Localhost streaming throughput reached 1.25 Gbps — approximately 3,000× the bandwidth required for real-time single-layer activation capture at generation speed. Thunderbolt 4 analysis confirms the link is never a bottleneck: even at peak Mac Studio inference throughput for a 34B model, TB4 utilization for full-depth capture stays below 1% of available bandwidth. Distributed SAE training (2 workers, 50,000 steps on Llama-3.2-3B) converged within 0.05% of single-node baseline MSE, with identical L₀ and fraction of variance explained (0.9822).

Cross-architecture feature matching across SAEs trained on Llama-3.2-3B (layer 14), Mistral-7B (layer 16), and Qwen2.5-3B (layer 18) identified 3,753 features shared across all three model families at cosine similarity ≥ 0.8 over a shared evaluation corpus. These three models share no publicly known training data, differ in architecture (GQA vs. full attention vs. sliding window), differ in scale (3B vs. 7B parameters), and were developed by different organizations. The convergence of SAE features from all three onto 3,753 common activation-pattern directions constitutes quantitative evidence for the Platonic Representation Hypothesis — that large networks trained on diverse text converge on a shared statistical model of language, independent of architectural choices.

**Safety classifier.** A feature-based safety classifier built on the Llama SAE achieved F1 = 0.519 on a 200-example balanced evaluation set, against a random baseline of 0.500. The failure is mechanistically understood rather than mysterious. Feature selection by activation frequency on the WikiText-103 training corpus identified 173 factual features, 24 stylistic features, and one military-history detector as the top-200 candidates — a distribution that reflects the labeling corpus, not safety relevance. The single nominally harmful feature fires on 9.9% of Wikipedia tokens (too broad) and fires specifically on historical military text (wrong domain). The diagnosis specifies three concrete corrections: replace the labeling corpus with a safety-targeted dataset (AdvBench or HarmBench), select features by differential activation frequency (harmful minus safe) rather than absolute frequency, and target later layers (20–28) where intent representations are more likely to be encoded. The harm scoring pipeline and evaluation infrastructure are reusable; the failure is in the input data design, not the pipeline logic.

## Research Vision

The three studies described in this application constitute a coherent arc across three levels of mechanistic interpretability: circuit-level causal analysis of specific behaviors, dictionary-level comparison of feature learning across training objectives, and infrastructure-level solutions for running these analyses on models too large for a single analysis node. Each study was motivated by a question the prior level left open, and each informs what the next level needs to do better.

The clearest thread is the infrastructure argument. Mechanistic interpretability has produced its most compelling results — IOI circuits, monosemantic features in MLP layers, the biology of Claude — on models in the 1B–7B range, using pipelines that assume GPU cluster access or single-node memory capacity sufficient for the full model. The studies in this application demonstrate that neither assumption is necessary: Apple Silicon provides up to 192 GB of unified memory and 800 GB/s memory bandwidth on a single chip, MLX enables efficient inference over models that previously required multi-GPU setups, and Thunderbolt 4 provides inter-node bandwidth thousands of times more than activation streaming requires. The technical barriers to interpretability research on 7B–34B models are lower than the field's infrastructure assumptions suggest.

The scientific argument is the universality result. 3,753 features shared across Llama-3.2-3B, Mistral-7B, and Qwen2.5-3B — at cosine similarity ≥ 0.8 over activation patterns on a shared corpus — means that interpretability findings are more portable than single-model studies suggest. If a circuit or feature is discovered in one model, the probability that a functional analog exists in models from other organizations is non-negligible. The 13–17% universality rate identified in the SAE comparison study, and the 80% depth-zone conservation rate in the circuit tracing study, both point toward a common representational vocabulary that language models learn because the training distribution demands it, not because any particular architecture was designed to represent it.

The immediate research agenda follows directly from the open problems these studies expose. First, the circuit tracing study established that layer 15 of Llama-3.2-3B hosts the dominant IOI bottleneck head (L15H20) and a dominant factual association head (L15H17) — two different heads in the same layer, responding to the same task-relevant depth zone but implementing different computations. Understanding what these heads read from and write to the residual stream requires connecting circuit analysis with SAE-based feature attribution: which SAE features does L15H20 read from the residual stream, and how do those features change as the circuit resolves which name to copy? This cross-level analysis — circuits at the head level, features at the representation level — is the natural next synthesis of the first two studies.

Second, the safety classifier failure motivates a specific repair that is now well-scoped: a contrastively labeled SAE evaluated on a balanced harmful/safe corpus, with features selected by differential activation rather than base-rate frequency, targeting later layers. The goal of F1 ≥ 0.80 at FPR ≤ 0.10 is achievable with no changes to the scoring pipeline. This is meaningful not primarily as a classifier but as a proof of concept for feature-grounded safety evaluation: a classifier that fails mechanistically, can be repaired mechanistically, and whose outputs are human-interpretable because each flag traces to a specific feature with a name and a set of activating examples.

Third, the distributed infrastructure enables experiments that were previously infeasible on consumer hardware: full-depth activation capture across all layers of a 7B model, which enables cross-layer circuit tracing at scale; online SAE training that eliminates the collect-then-train round-trip and reduces the hypothesis-to-result cycle from hours to minutes; and eventually a "universal SAE" whose dictionary is initialized from the cross-architecture matched features and fine-tuned jointly on activations from multiple model families — a strong test of whether the universal features are truly shared representations or artifacts of the evaluation corpus.

The long-term goal is not any individual result but a change in the infrastructure assumptions of interpretability research. The science of understanding what large language models represent — the computations they perform, the features they track, the circuits that implement safety-relevant behaviors — should not require access to expensive centralized compute. The work in this application demonstrates that it does not.
