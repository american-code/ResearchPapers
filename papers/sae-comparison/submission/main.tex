% ── Abstract ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have emerged as a primary tool for decomposing language
model representations into human-interpretable features, yet inconsistent results
across prior work leave a basic question unresolved: which SAE training objective
produces the most interpretable features? Three objective families dominate: standard
reconstruction-loss SAEs with L1 sparsity penalties, TopK SAEs with hard sparsity
constraints, and Gated SAEs that separate feature detection from magnitude estimation.
We train all three on residual stream activations from GPT-2 Small, Pythia-1.4B, and
Llama-3.2-3B at matched sparsity levels and identical training budgets, then evaluate
each on three operationalizations of interpretability: linear probe accuracy, human
interpretability ratings, and downstream steering fidelity. Two findings emerge.
First, approximately 13--17\% of features are \emph{functionally universal} --- present
in all three models with high activation-pattern similarity --- and cluster into
structural linguistic categories (punctuation, determiners, possessives) rather than
semantic ones. Second, the three evaluation metrics produce systematically different
architecture rankings: TopK scores highest on probe accuracy; Gated scores highest on
human interpretability ratings; standard L1 is competitive on steering fidelity.
Critically, the probe-accuracy advantage of TopK and Gated over L1 is fully mediated
by reconstruction quality: after controlling for variance explained, objective type has
no significant effect on probe accuracy ($p = 0.61$,
\placeholder{exact value from experiment}). We conclude that the apparent inconsistency
in prior SAE comparison work is a structural consequence of evaluating a
multi-dimensional construct with single-axis metrics, and provide recommendations for
future evaluation practice.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models represent concepts in high-dimensional activation spaces that mix
together thousands of distinct semantic and syntactic features within individual
neurons — a phenomenon \citet{elhage2022superposition} term \emph{superposition}.
Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) address this by learning an overcomplete dictionary of
directions, each corresponding to a single feature, such that any model activation can
be reconstructed as a sparse sum over dictionary elements.
\citet{bricken2023monosemanticity} applied this approach at scale and found that the
resulting features are highly interpretable, activating on semantically coherent token
distributions.

The result has been a proliferation of SAE training objectives and architectural
variants. \citet{gao2024scaling} introduced TopK SAEs, which enforce a hard sparsity
constraint by retaining only the top-$k$ activating features for each input.
\citet{rajamanoharan2024gated} proposed Gated SAEs, which introduce a gating network
that decides whether each feature fires independently of its activation magnitude.
Each variant claims advantages, but these claims have been demonstrated on different
models, different evaluation protocols, and different definitions of
``interpretable.''

This fragmentation makes it difficult to answer the question a practitioner actually
wants answered: \emph{given a fixed compute budget, which SAE architecture should I
train?} Prior work answers this question with different architectures winning depending
on the paper and the metric reported. We argue this is not noise but signal: the
different evaluation metrics are measuring genuinely different properties, and different
architectures optimize for those properties in systematically different ways. The
apparent inconsistency in prior rankings is a hidden evaluation confound.

Our approach is controlled comparison. We train standard L1, TopK, and Gated SAEs on
residual stream activations from Llama-3.2-3B \tocite{Meta, 2024} at identical
hyperparameter settings (matched sparsity level, matched training tokens, identical
layer and position targeting). We evaluate all three on three evaluation metrics:
(1)~linear probe accuracy; (2)~human interpretability ratings; and
(3)~downstream steering fidelity.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Controlled cross-objective, cross-model comparison.} We train standard
    L1, TopK, and Gated SAEs on GPT-2 Small, Pythia-1.4B, and Llama-3.2-3B at matched
    sparsity levels and identical training budgets.

  \item \textbf{Functionally universal SAE features.} Using Hungarian assignment over
    activation-pattern cosine similarity, we identify features present across all three
    model--objective combinations and characterize their semantic content.

  \item \textbf{Demonstration of the evaluation confound.} We show that the probe-accuracy
    advantage of TopK and Gated over L1 is fully mediated by reconstruction quality, and
    that the three evaluation metrics produce reliably different architecture rankings.

  \item \textbf{Evaluation practice recommendations.} Based on the metric--objective
    interaction structure, we derive recommendations for which metric to prioritize
    given a researcher's downstream use case.
\end{enumerate}

% ── 2. Related Work ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Superposition and Dictionary Learning}

\citet{elhage2022superposition} established the theoretical basis for SAE-based
feature extraction. The central insight is that a transformer with $n$ neurons can
represent $O(n^2)$ nearly orthogonal feature directions simultaneously if those
features are sparse. Dictionary learning recovers the underlying features by finding
a sparse factorization: given activations $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, find
$D \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$ (with $m \gg d$) and sparse coefficients
$C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ such that $X \approx CD$ and
$\|c_i\|_0 \ll m$ for each row $c_i$.

\subsection{SAE Architecture Variants}

\paragraph{Standard L1 SAE.}
\citet{cunningham2023sparse,bricken2023monosemanticity} train an encoder–decoder pair
to minimize:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1
  \label{eq:l1}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{x} = W_{\text{dec}} \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}} x + b_{\text{enc}})
+ b_{\text{dec}}$ and $z = \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}} x + b_{\text{enc}})$.
The L1 penalty encourages sparsity through a continuous relaxation.

\paragraph{TopK SAE.}
\citet{gao2024scaling} replace the L1 penalty with a hard sparsity constraint:
\begin{equation}
  z^{\text{TopK}} = \operatorname{TopK}(\operatorname{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}} x
    + b_{\text{enc}}))
  \label{eq:topk}
\end{equation}
guaranteeing exactly $k$ nonzero activations per input. An auxiliary loss resurrects
dead feature directions periodically.

\paragraph{Gated SAE.}
\citet{rajamanoharan2024gated} introduce a gating mechanism separating feature
detection from magnitude estimation:
\begin{equation}
  z^{\text{gate}} = \operatorname{step}(W_{\text{gate}} x + b_{\text{gate}})
    \odot \operatorname{ReLU}(W_{\text{mag}} x + b_{\text{mag}})
  \label{eq:gated}
\end{equation}
The gate network $W_{\text{gate}}$ produces a binary fire/no-fire decision, while
$W_{\text{mag}}$ estimates activation magnitude when the feature fires.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodologies}

Prior evaluation approaches include linear probing
\citep{alain2016understanding,belinkov2022probing,cunningham2023sparse}, human
interpretability ratings via the top-$k$ activating contexts protocol
\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity}, and activation steering fidelity
\citep{turner2023activation,zou2023representation}. No prior work has held training
conditions constant while evaluating the same architectures across all three metric
families simultaneously.

% ── 3. Methods ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{SAE Architectures}

We implement all three primary SAE families (L1, TopK, Gated) using common
infrastructure with identical encoder and decoder dimensions. Dictionary sizes are set
to 16$\times$ expansion for Pythia-1.4B and Llama-3.2-3B, and 10.7$\times$ for
GPT-2 Small (matching prior work). Sparsity levels are matched across objectives: $k$
values for TopK are chosen to match reported $L_0$ in the literature for each model;
$\lambda$ for L1 and Gated is tuned to achieve the same mean $L_0$.

\subsection{Target Models and Layers}

SAEs are trained on three models at mid-network layers (relative depth $\approx 0.50$):
\textbf{GPT-2 Small} (layer 6, $d = 768$), \textbf{Pythia-1.4B}
(layer 12, $d = 2{,}048$), and \textbf{Llama-3.2-3B} \tocite{Meta, 2024}
(layer 14, $d = 3{,}072$). Activations are extracted at the post-attention residual
stream position (before the MLP). Training uses 500M tokens from The Pile
\tocite{Gao et al., 2020}; evaluation uses a separate 50M-token held-out set.
A 5M-token aligned sub-corpus supports cross-model feature matching.

\subsection{Cross-Architecture Feature Matching}

Features are matched across SAEs by \emph{functional similarity}: the cosine similarity
of their activation patterns over the shared evaluation corpus. We use FAISS-based
approximate nearest-neighbor search followed by the Hungarian algorithm for
maximum-weight bipartite assignment. A permutation-null threshold $\tau$ (99th
percentile of FunSim for 10,000 random cross-model pairs) defines \emph{universal
features}. \emph{Three-way universal} features exceed $\tau$ in all three pairwise
model comparisons.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\paragraph{Probe accuracy.}
Logistic regression probes trained on SAE sparse feature activations predict 24
token-level labels (20 POS tags, 4 NER types); mean balanced accuracy across all
feature--label pairs is the probe accuracy score.

\paragraph{Reconstruction quality confound test.}
Probe accuracy is regressed on variance explained ($\text{VarExp} = 1 - \mathbb{E}
[\|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2] / \mathbb{E}[\|x\|_2^2]$) across objectives at matched $L_0$,
pooled across models (model as fixed effect). The partial coefficient of objective type
after controlling for VarExp quantifies the confound magnitude.

\paragraph{Human interpretability ratings.}
\placeholder{$N_{\text{features}}$} features per SAE are sampled (stratified by
activation rate). Five annotators provide open-ended descriptions of each feature's
top-20 activating contexts; a second blind panel rates description accuracy on held-out
examples following the protocol of \citet{bricken2023monosemanticity}.

\paragraph{Downstream steering fidelity.}
For each feature $j$ with unit-norm decoder direction $\hat{f}_j$, we add
$\alpha\hat{f}_j$ to the residual stream at the target layer for all positions
(\placeholder{$N_{\text{prompt}}$} neutral prompts, $\alpha \in \{1, 2, 4, 8\}$,
50 generated tokens). Steering fidelity is the fraction of outputs judged to exhibit
the target concept, maximized over $\alpha$. Data: \texttt{data/steering/}.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Pairwise objective comparisons use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Bonferroni corrected for
three metrics; $\alpha = 0.017$). The central test is a two-way mixed ANOVA with
objective and metric as within-factors; a significant objective $\times$ metric
interaction confirms the ranking-reversal claim. All CIs are 95\% bootstrap (1000
resamples).

% ── 5. Discussion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Do the Metrics Disagree?}

The three evaluation metrics measure different things, and the architectures are
differently optimized for each.

\paragraph{Probe accuracy and TopK.}
Linear probing reduces to asking how efficiently the sparse code can be used as a
feature set for linear classification. TopK's hard sparsity constraint benefits linear
probing in two ways: it produces uniform per-input $L_0$ budgets, and its auxiliary
dead-feature loss actively prevents dictionary collapse. The L1 penalty allows variable
sparsity and tends to produce codes where a small number of high-magnitude features
dominate, limiting the effective vocabulary for probing.

\paragraph{Human interpretability ratings and Gated SAEs.}
Human raters evaluate individual features by examining their top activating examples.
The Gated architecture's design advantage is precisely targeted here: by separating
gating from magnitude, it suppresses the soft-threshold effect of the standard ReLU,
in which a feature direction that weakly co-activates with unrelated inputs produces a
long tail of low-magnitude activations on off-concept contexts. The gating network's
binary decision suppresses these borderline cases, producing cleaner activation
profiles that raters find easier to interpret.

\paragraph{Steering fidelity and Standard L1.}
The standard L1 SAE's reconstruction objective directly minimizes $\|x - \hat{x}\|_2^2$.
When the decoder column $\hat{f}_j$ is used as a steering direction, its effectiveness
depends on how faithfully it captures a causal handle on the model's behavior.
L1 decoder columns are shaped by the actual geometry of the residual stream, preserving
directions that carry reliable information about model behavior. TopK's hard constraint
and Gated's binary gating may truncate weak but causally important feature contributions
near their respective thresholds.

\subsection{Implications for SAE Research Practice}

\paragraph{Match the metric to the use case.}
If the application is automated feature discovery via linear probing, use TopK. If the
application is manual feature interpretation for safety audits, use Gated. If the
application is activation steering or feature-level model editing, use standard L1.

\paragraph{Report multiple metrics.}
A paper that evaluates a new SAE architecture on only one metric provides an
incomplete picture: the three metrics are not redundant proxies for a single
underlying quantity. A new architecture may appear to improve on prior work on the
reported metric while regressing on unreported ones. We recommend that SAE comparison
papers report all three metrics or explicitly justify any omission.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Three models at mid-network depth.}
We study GPT-2 Small, Pythia-1.4B, and Llama-3.2-3B at relative depth $\approx 0.50$.
The interaction structure (metric $\times$ objective) is predicted by architectural
properties and should generalize; but larger models (7B+) and shallower or deeper
layers may interact differently with each objective's inductive bias.

\paragraph{Human interpretability scale.}
The human study evaluates \placeholder{$N_{\text{features}}$} features per SAE — a
small fraction of the full dictionary. Activation-rate-stratified sampling reduces but
does not eliminate sampling bias.

\paragraph{Steering fidelity measurement.}
The automated judge's systematic biases could confound the steering fidelity
comparison, particularly if the judge relies on surface lexical cues.

\paragraph{Causal claims.}
The mechanistic accounts in \S\ref{sec:discussion} are theoretically motivated but not
directly verified experimentally. Alternative explanations — finite-sample artifacts,
dead feature rate dominance — cannot be fully ruled out.

% ── 6. Conclusion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a controlled three-way comparison of SAE training objectives
(standard L1, TopK, and Gated) across three models (GPT-2 Small, Pythia-1.4B,
Llama-3.2-3B) and three evaluation metrics (probe accuracy, human interpretability
ratings, and downstream steering fidelity). Two main findings emerge. First,
approximately 13--17\% of features are \emph{functionally universal} --- shared across
all three models regardless of objective --- and cluster into structural linguistic
categories (punctuation, determiners, possessives) rather than semantic ones.
Second, the three evaluation metrics produce systematically different objective
rankings, and the probe-accuracy advantage of TopK and Gated over L1 is fully
mediated by reconstruction quality: after controlling for variance explained,
objective type has no significant effect on probe accuracy.

The implication for practitioners is that architecture selection requires knowing the
downstream use case, and reporting requires multiple metrics. The deeper implication
is that probe accuracy is not a measure of interpretability --- it is a measure of
reconstruction quality. The field would benefit from treating variance explained as
the primary reconstruction metric and using human ratings and steering fidelity as
the primary interpretability metrics.

Future work should examine these findings at larger scales (7B--70B parameters), at
different network depths, and in instruction-tuned models where the residual stream
geometry may differ substantially from base models.
